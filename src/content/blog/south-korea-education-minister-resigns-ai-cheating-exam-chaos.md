# South Korea's Education Minister Resigns After AI Cheating Rules Disrupt University Entrance Exam

South Korea's Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin resigned on November 22, 2026, after last-minute measures designed to prevent AI-assisted cheating caused confusion around the country's national university entrance examination.

President Lee Jae Myung accepted the resignation, according to Yonhap News Agency. The controversy centred on rules and procedures announced close to the examination, leaving students and teachers uncertain about what would be allowed and how the safeguards would be enforced.

The episode shows how quickly generative AI is forcing examination authorities to reconsider the way they protect high-stakes tests.

## What happened?

South Korea's College Scholastic Ability Test, commonly known as the Suneung or CSAT, was held on Thursday, November 19, 2026.

In the days before the examination, authorities introduced measures intended to prevent candidates from using electronic devices or concealed technology to obtain assistance.

The changes drew criticism because of their timing and implementation.

According to Yonhap, the measures included:

- Allowing candidates to bring only pencils into examination rooms.
- Covering buttons on students' clothing with stickers to prevent the use of miniature cameras.
- Sending emergency mobile alerts during the examination to identify candidates who had not handed in their phones.

The Education Ministry reported 111 suspected cheating cases involving electronic devices during the examination.

## Why did the minister resign?

Choi said he accepted responsibility for the lack of preparation ahead of the examination. Yonhap reported that he acknowledged the inconvenience caused to students and the public by the changes announced just before the test.

President Lee accepted the resignation. The presidential office said the prime minister would take responsibility for follow-up measures while the process to nominate a successor proceeded.

The controversy was not simply about whether authorities could detect AI-assisted cheating. It was also about whether students had been given clear and stable instructions before an examination that carries major consequences for university admission.

## Why is AI changing examination security?

Generative AI creates new ways for candidates to obtain assistance. A device can potentially capture a question, send it to an AI system and return an answer. Miniature cameras, connected devices and concealed communication equipment complicate the work of invigilators.

But technology is only one part of the challenge. Examination authorities also need rules that are understandable, enforceable and communicated well in advance.

| Traditional exam control | New concern raised by AI |
|---|---|
| Confiscating phones | Hidden or secondary connected devices |
| Checking calculators and watches | Devices with AI or network capabilities |
| Monitoring candidate behaviour | External AI assistance through concealed hardware |
| Prohibiting communication | Fast transfer of images and answers |
| Investigating suspicious answers | Distinguishing independent work from AI-generated assistance |

These are general risks associated with AI-enabled cheating. They should not be interpreted as evidence that every suspected case in the South Korean examination involved an AI system.

## The policy problem: security without creating chaos

A strong examination policy needs to balance three requirements.

**Clear rules:** Candidates must know what is permitted before they enter the examination centre.

**Consistent enforcement:** Invigilators need procedures that can be applied uniformly across thousands of rooms.

**Proportionate safeguards:** Security measures should address credible risks without introducing unnecessary disruption or uncertainty.

The South Korean episode demonstrates that a technically motivated rule can still fail as an administrative measure if it arrives too late or is poorly explained.

## What remains unclear?

The resignation does not resolve the broader question of how South Korea will redesign examination security for future tests. The cited reports do not establish how many of the 111 suspected cases involved AI specifically, nor do they provide a final breakdown of the cases.

The government has said follow-up measures will be considered. Details of any permanent examination policy changes remain to be confirmed.
