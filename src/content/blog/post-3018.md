Alright, let’s talk about India’s new draft AI policy.

It’s finally here, a 7-pillar framework, a ₹2,000 crore investment plan, talks of homegrown AI models, ethical audits, compute infrastructure, and even a full-blown Center of Excellence. On the surface, it looks impressive. Like, really well-packaged.

But let’s pause for a second.

The real question is: Is this a serious, forward-thinking policy built to shape the future of AI in India, or is this just a dressed-up version of what we already had, sprinkled with AI buzzwords?

Let’s break it down together.

## First, What’s in the Draft?

Here’s what the policy claims to focus on:

*   **Compute Infrastructure** – Setting up a national AI grid and compute power for Indian researchers and startups.
*   **Data Ecosystem** – Emphasizing federated learning, privacy-first frameworks, and creating “data trusts.”
*   **Local AI Models** – Support for building Indian-language LLMs, and more funding for open-source AI tools.
*   **Skilling and Talent** – Upskilling engineers, researchers, and launching formal AI programs in universities.
*   **Ethics and Regulation** – Algorithm audits, identifying “high-risk AI” systems, building transparency into models.
*   **Startup Ecosystem** – Accelerators, grants, and compute credits for early-stage AI founders.
*   **Oversight and Governance** – A distributed model where different government bodies regulate AI in their own sectors.

Looks solid, right?

But let’s not confuse _structure_ for _impact_.

## Where It Actually Shines

Let’s give credit where it’s due.

### 1\. Finally, Some Real Money on the Table

That ₹2,000 crore investment is a strong signal that India is ready to compete on AI infrastructure. This isn't just about building models, it’s about giving researchers and developers the compute muscle to train them. A lot of folks don’t realize how expensive GPUs and large-scale model training really are.

This money could mean more Indian LLMs, open benchmarks, and compute access for folks who’ve been priced out of the game so far.

### 2\. Not Just Tech; There’s an Ethics Lens

The draft puts a lot of emphasis on responsible AI, especially for high-risk systems like healthcare or policing. Algorithmic audits, impact assessments, and risk-based categorization are a much-needed addition to the conversation.

We’ve seen what happens when innovation outpaces accountability. This framework at least acknowledges that.

### 3\. A Whole-of-Government Approach

This one’s subtle but important. Rather than creating one new AI regulator, the policy allows existing regulators (like RBI for finance, TRAI for telecom, etc.) to shape AI norms in their own domains. That’s smart; you can’t expect one agency to understand the nuances of every industry.

## Now, Where It Still Falls Short

For all its ambition, there are a few things that still feel half-baked.

### 1\. What Does “High-Risk” Even Mean?

The draft mentions high-risk use cases a lot, but it doesn’t really define them. Are LLMs in education high-risk? Is facial recognition in railway stations? We need clearer, actionable definitions here.

Otherwise, it’s just regulatory grey space.

### 2\. Too Much Leaning on Existing Laws

The draft depends heavily on the existing Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDP) and the IT Act. But here’s the thing, those laws weren’t written with AI in mind. They’re like using building codes from 1990 to design a smart home in 2025.

AI needs its own guardrails, especially around explainability, hallucinations, bias, and model security.

### 3\. Still a Bit Too Cozy with Big Tech

There’s a strong push for self-regulation and voluntary standards. And while that sounds good in theory, let’s be honest, if this space is only shaped by large tech firms, we risk locking out startups, researchers, and civil society from the table.

India’s AI policy needs to be people-first, not corporate-first.

## Who Should Pay Attention and Why

### If You’re a Founder or Builder

There’s opportunity here — infrastructure access, grants, government partnerships. But stay sharp. The success of this policy will depend on whether smaller players actually get access, or if it’s another case of “big names only.”

### If You’re a Researcher or Academic

The proposed Centers of Excellence and national compute grid could be game-changers. But will access be equitable? Or is it limited to IITs and elite labs? Keep an eye on who’s actually invited in.

### If You’re a Citizen or User

You may not think this affects you, but it does. How your data is collected, what decisions AI systems make about you, whether you can contest those decisions, that’s all in play. The stakes are high, and we need clear, enforceable rights.

## So, What Needs to Happen Next?

This is the part where policy becomes real. Here’s what to watch out for:

*   **Define high-risk use cases clearly** and enforce them across both public and private sectors.
*   **Make algorithmic audits mandatory and public.** It’s not enough to say, “we did an audit.” We need to know the results.
*   **Open access to compute and data** so AI development isn’t limited to just a few institutions.
*   **Create feedback loops with civil society and startups.** Policy made in closed rooms will always miss ground realities.
*   **Track progress publicly.** Release metrics. How many audits were done? How many startups got funded? How many models were open-sourced?

## My Takes

Look, the draft AI policy is a solid start. The intent feels serious. The ambitions are in the right direction.

But policies don’t change ecosystems; implementation does.

And in a space as fast-moving as AI, we can’t afford to sit back and just “watch it unfold.” We need open debate, independent oversight, and strong engagement from developers, researchers, users, and yes, even skeptics.

India can lead in AI. But it won’t be because of flashy PDFs or seven-pillar infographics. It’ll be because we made the right calls now when it still mattered.

Let’s make sure this isn’t just a rebranding exercise. Let’s push for reform that actually reforms.
