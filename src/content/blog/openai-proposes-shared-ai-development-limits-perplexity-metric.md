# OpenAI Proposes Shared Limits on AI Development. Can the Industry Agree on a Common Safety Threshold?

OpenAI has proposed a shared way for leading AI developers to measure progress and agree on limits for increasingly capable models. In an essay published on November 22, 2026, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki argues that developers should track a measure called perplexity and use it, alongside other evaluations, to inform common safety standards.

The proposal is significant because it moves the discussion beyond broad commitments to responsible AI. It asks whether competing AI companies can agree on measurable thresholds that determine when development should pause until monitoring and safety systems improve.

This is a proposal, not a policy already adopted by the industry.

## What exactly has OpenAI proposed?

Pachocki argues that the progress of modern AI systems can be understood partly through how effectively a model predicts data it has not seen. He proposes perplexity, or the closely related measure cross-entropy loss, as one metric that could help compare pretraining progress across developers.

His suggested framework has two linked parts:

1. Establish common measurements that allow developers to compare progress.
2. Agree on development thresholds that companies do not cross until specified safety and monitoring milestones have been achieved.

The second part is the more consequential. A shared metric would help identify when systems are approaching a threshold. A shared commitment would define what developers should do when they reach it.

## What is perplexity?

Perplexity measures how well a language model predicts the next token in a sequence. A token can be a word, part of a word or another unit of text.

In general, lower perplexity means the model assigns higher probability to the actual next token and therefore predicts the evaluated text more accurately.

| Concept | Plain English explanation |
|---|---|
| Token | A small unit of text processed by a language model |
| Prediction | The model estimates what token comes next |
| Perplexity | A measure of how uncertain the model is about the next token |
| Lower perplexity | Better prediction on the evaluated dataset |
| Cross-entropy loss | A closely related measure commonly used during model training |

Perplexity is not a complete measure of intelligence, safety or real-world usefulness. A model can perform well on a prediction benchmark and still fail at reasoning, tool use, reliability or safe behaviour.

Pachocki acknowledges that comparisons across laboratories are difficult because companies use different datasets and training mixtures. He argues that shared evaluation and greater transparency could make the metric more useful.

## How would the proposed limits work?

The idea is to define a first development threshold and identify the safety capabilities that must exist before developers move beyond it.

| Stage | Proposed mechanism | What it would establish |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Measurement | Compare models using shared metrics | A common view of capability progress |
| 2. Threshold | Set a development limit | A point that participating developers agree not to cross |
| 3. Safety milestone | Define monitoring and evaluation requirements | Evidence needed before the limit can be raised |
| 4. Review | Assess whether the milestone has been met | A basis for changing the threshold |
| 5. Successive limits | Repeat the process as capabilities advance | A continuing governance framework |

Pachocki gives the development of a successor to chain-of-thought monitors as an example of a concrete milestone. The essay does not provide a finished industry rulebook or a final list of universally accepted safety tests.

## Why does OpenAI think the issue is becoming urgent?

The essay argues that AI may increasingly contribute to the research that produces better AI systems. Pachocki says meaningful design contributions from AI have already appeared in recent model development and warns that AI could drive major advances in training methods as early as 2027.

The concern is about the relative speed of two processes: capability development and the development of tools that can understand, evaluate and monitor those capabilities.

If capability improvements accelerate faster than safety evaluation, developers may find it harder to understand what a new system can do before deploying it.

Reuters reported that OpenAI CEO Sam Altman endorsed the proposal and called for common limits across the industry.

## The difficult questions the proposal leaves open

A common metric sounds straightforward until competing laboratories try to use it.

| Open question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Which datasets should be used? | Different data mixtures can produce incomparable scores |
| Can one metric represent broad capability? | Prediction quality does not capture every useful or dangerous capability |
| Who defines the thresholds? | Companies may disagree about acceptable risk |
| Who verifies compliance? | A voluntary standard needs credible independent checks |
| What happens when a company does not participate? | A shared limit is weaker if major developers remain outside it |
| How are safety milestones tested? | Monitoring claims need repeatable evidence |

There is also a distinction between measuring a model and deciding what a company should be allowed to build. The first is a technical exercise. The second involves governance, incentives and accountability.

## What is confirmed and what is not?

| Confirmed | Not established by the announcement |
|---|---|
| Pachocki published the proposal on November 22, 2026 | That other leading AI labs have accepted it |
| Perplexity is proposed as one shared metric | That perplexity alone measures intelligence or risk |
| The proposal includes common development limits | That a binding industry standard exists |
| OpenAI says AI may accelerate AI research | A precise date for a capability breakthrough |

## The central question

The proposal's value will depend less on whether one company publishes a thoughtful essay and more on whether competing developers can agree to transparent measurements, meaningful safety milestones and credible ways to verify them.

A common metric can make progress easier to compare. A common limit requires something harder: companies accepting constraints even when competitors may choose to move faster.
