**SHOULD YOU BOTHER?**

### Everyone's telling you to add "llms.txt" and Content-Signal headers to your website. Google says don't bother.

A simple explanation of two trendy "AI SEO" tools, what a real test found, and the one-line answer to whether you should use them.

**THE SHORT ANSWER**

**Skip them. They don't help right now — and Google confirmed it.**

llms.txt and Content-Signal headers are small text files you add to your website, meant to "tell" AI tools things like "please don't use my content to train your model" or "here's who I am." The idea sounds smart. The problem: almost no AI tool actually reads these files today. So adding them doesn't hurt you, but it also does nothing. Your time is better spent elsewhere — we'll show you where, below.

* * *

### What even are these two things?

**llms.txt** is like a small note file you place on your website. It's supposed to tell AI tools (like ChatGPT or Gemini) important things about your business or you, in simple sentences — a shortcut so the AI doesn't have to "read" your whole site to understand it.

**Content-Signal headers** are a setting inside a file called robots.txt (a file websites use to tell search engines what they're allowed to do). This newer setting lets you say things like "you can show my content in search, but don't use it to train your AI model."

Both sound like good ideas on paper. Lots of SEO tools and blogs have been telling people to add them. So someone actually decided to test it.

* * *

### Someone had a good reason to test this — and did

An SEO business owner in Brazil has a problem: two other well-known people share his exact name — a government official and a news reporter. Because of this, AI tools kept mixing him up with them when people searched about him.

So he tried two things, to see if either one would help AI tools understand who he actually is:

**Step 1: Added an llms.txt file about himself**  
Wrote out his job, his company, his city, and what he does — in simple sentences, hoping AI tools would read it and understand who he is.

**Step 2: Turned on Content-Signal headers**  
Set his website to say "yes, show me in search, but no, don't use my content to train your AI" — just to see if stating it clearly made any difference.

He noticed his description on tools like Gemini and Perplexity got a bit more accurate over a couple of months. But he was honest: he couldn't be sure if that was because of these two changes, or just because more people had linked to and mentioned him online over time anyway.

* * *

### Google's own spokesperson settled it

John Mueller works at Google and regularly answers real SEO questions in public. He replied directly to this test, and did not hold back:

> "Google doesn't use llms.txt... none of the AI tools use these 'content-signal' settings either. It doesn't do anything. It just adds extra clutter to your website file, and more work for you to maintain it."  
> — John Mueller, Google

In simple terms: think of these files like putting up a sign written in a language nobody nearby speaks yet. It doesn't confuse anyone, but it doesn't help anyone either — because right now, no AI tool is trained to look for that sign.

* * *

### If a text file doesn't help AI understand you, what does?

People in the same discussion made a really useful point: AI tools don't trust things you say about yourself. They trust things other people say about you.

**Doesn't help much:**

*   Writing an llms.txt file about yourself
*   Turning on Content-Signal headers
*   Filling out website "About" tags with no outside proof

**Actually helps:**

*   Getting mentioned or written about on other websites
*   A clear, filled-out LinkedIn profile
*   Being quoted in an article, interview, or event bio
*   Linking all your profiles together so they clearly point to one person — you

Think of it like a job reference. You can write your own reference letter saying you're great at your job — nobody's going to fully believe that. But if three other companies say the same thing about you, it starts to feel true. AI tools work the same way.

* * *

### Simple next steps

**In order of what matters most:**

1.  **Pick one main profile** as your home base — LinkedIn works well for most people.
2.  **Make sure your other profiles link back to it** — Twitter/X, Instagram, your website's About page.
3.  **Try to get mentioned outside your own website** — a guest post, a podcast, a quote in someone else's article. This matters far more than any text file.
4.  **Keep your website's basic details filled in properly** (this is what "schema" means) — but treat it as backup, not the main fix.
5.  **Don't waste time on llms.txt or Content-Signal headers for now.** They won't hurt you, but right now, no major AI tool is confirmed to actually read them.

The bigger lesson here: a lot of "AI SEO" advice going around right now is really just "write a file about yourself." That's not what convinces an AI tool. What convinces it is other people, on other websites, saying the same thing about you. That takes longer to build — but it's the part that's actually proven to work.
