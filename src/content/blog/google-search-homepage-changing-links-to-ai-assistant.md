For years, the Google homepage was almost boring by design. A logo, a search box and two buttons. You typed a few words, pressed Enter and received a page of links. Google's job was to help you find the right website.

That familiar page is changing.

The latest Google homepage is beginning to look less like a doorway to the web and more like a place where you can ask an AI assistant to do things. Shortcuts for creating images, asking questions about files and brainstorming sit alongside AI Mode in the search box.

The change is bigger than a redesign. It reflects a shift in what Google wants Search to do: not only find pages that may contain an answer, but help users explore a question, ask follow-ups and complete more involved tasks.

## From Google! in 1998 to the familiar search box

The 1998 Google homepage looked very different from the clean white page most people remember. It carried the Google! logo with a BETA label, a search box, two buttons and additional links.

The basic idea was simple. Enter a search term and Google would return websites that matched it. The user opened those pages, compared information and decided which source was useful.

By 2010, the homepage had become much simpler. The Google logo, a large search field and the familiar Search and I'm Feeling Lucky buttons dominated the page. The interface was deliberately quiet. It gave the search box almost all the attention.

By 2015, the logo had changed to the cleaner sans-serif design, but the central interaction remained familiar. Search was still a box into which people typed keywords. The results page did most of the work after that.

The supplied historical timeline image is a useful visual reference for this evolution. The point is not that the homepage changed every year. It is that the search box remained the central interaction for a long time.

## Search became more than ten blue links

The results page did not remain a simple list of links. Google added images, maps, news, videos, shopping results, knowledge panels, local information and other specialised formats.

Google increasingly tried to answer parts of a query directly on the results page, while still giving users routes to websites and sources.

AI has accelerated that change.

Google introduced AI Overviews to provide AI-generated summaries for some searches. It then introduced AI Mode as a more conversational experience for questions that need deeper reasoning, multiple steps or follow-up questions.

In May 2025, Google described AI Mode as its most powerful AI search experience, with the ability to ask follow-up questions and explore helpful links from the web. The company said people were asking longer, more complex and multimodal questions.

Instead of entering one keyword phrase, opening a result, returning to Google and trying another query, a person could ask a longer question and continue refining it in the same AI conversation.

## The homepage is putting AI actions in front of users

The latest design makes that direction visible before a user even starts typing.

The homepage includes shortcuts such as:

- **Create images:** Start an image-generation task.
- **Ask about files:** Upload a file and ask questions about its contents.
- **Brainstorm:** Use an AI-led interaction to develop ideas.
- **AI Mode:** Enter a conversational search experience directly from the search box.

Google's Search leadership has described these buttons as ways to help people discover more things they can do with Search. The traditional search box remains. Users can still type a query and press Enter to see regular Search results.

That distinction matters. Google has not simply switched off traditional web search. It is changing the prominence and visibility of the options around it.

## Google wants you to continue the conversation

The next change is not just about starting an AI search. It is about returning to one.

Google's VP of Product for Search, Robby Stein, announced a collapsible sidebar on the desktop homepage in November. It gives users a way to find and reopen recent AI searches.

The reasoning is revealing. People are coming to Search with questions that are not one and done. Users may begin with one question, ask for clarification, add constraints, compare options and continue exploring.

The sidebar is designed to make it easier to pick up where a previous AI search ended. It can make Search feel more like an ongoing workspace than a one-time query box.

The feature is rolling out on desktop. Availability may vary while the rollout progresses.

## What is changing in the way people search?

Consider someone planning a family trip.

Earlier, the person might search for places to visit in Kerala, open several websites, search for hotels, check distances and run another search for suitable activities.

With AI Mode, the user can begin with a longer request: "Plan a five-day Kerala trip for two adults and one child, with nature, food and activities, but avoid long drives."

The user can then ask follow-up questions: "Make it cheaper", "Replace the beach day" or "Compare these two routes".

The difference is not that Google has suddenly invented questions. People have always asked complex questions. The difference is that the interface is designed to keep the context of the earlier question and support a continuing exchange.

## Does this mean Google is becoming a chatbot?

Partly, but that description misses an important point.

Google still has a web index, ranking systems, crawling infrastructure and traditional results pages. AI Mode is another way to interact with information from the web, not proof that the underlying search engine has disappeared.

The more useful way to understand the change is that Google is bringing together three experiences:

1. **Traditional search:** Find pages, websites, products and sources.
2. **AI answers:** Get a generated response with links and supporting information.
3. **Conversational search:** Ask follow-up questions and continue exploring a topic without starting over.

The homepage is increasingly making all three feel like parts of one product.

## What this means for websites, publishers and SEO

This change matters to anyone who depends on Google for discovery.

In a traditional search journey, a person often clicked a result to read an article, compare products or find a service. In an AI-led journey, some of the initial explanation may be provided directly in the search experience. The user may click through when they need a source, deeper detail, a product page or a next step.

That does not make websites irrelevant. It changes the reasons people may visit them.

For publishers and businesses, the practical priorities are:

- Publish original information that adds value beyond a generic summary.
- Make important facts, authorship, dates and sources clear.
- Organise pages so people and search systems can understand the subject.
- Provide useful evidence, examples, data and first-hand experience.
- Build content that answers the next question as well as the first one.
- Track visibility and qualified visits separately from raw clicks.

A page that merely repeats information available everywhere may be easier for an AI system to summarise without sending a user to the original website. A page with original reporting, expert explanation, useful tools, strong evidence or a genuinely helpful service gives people a stronger reason to visit.

This is a strategic implication, not a guarantee about how Google will rank or cite any particular page.

## The search box is still there. Its job is changing.

The Google homepage has moved through several visual identities, but for most of its history its central promise was consistent: type something and Google will help you find it.

Now the company is asking users to do more. Ask a longer question. Upload a file. Create an image. Brainstorm. Continue a previous AI search.

The most important change is not the disappearance of a button or the addition of a sidebar. It is the shift in the expected behaviour of the user.

Google is moving from helping people find a page that might contain an answer towards helping them work through the question itself.

The web is still part of that experience. But the homepage is increasingly designed around the conversation that happens before, during and after someone visits a website.
