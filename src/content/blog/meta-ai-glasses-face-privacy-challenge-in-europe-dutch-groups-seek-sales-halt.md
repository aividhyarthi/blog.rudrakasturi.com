AI glasses are designed to make technology less visible. A camera, microphone and assistant can sit inside an ordinary-looking pair of frames, allowing the wearer to record, ask questions or interact with an AI system without holding a phone.

That convenience is also creating a difficult privacy question: what happens to the people being recorded?

Dutch consumer and digital rights groups asked a court on November 26, 2026, to halt sales of Meta's AI-powered smart glasses in the Netherlands. Reuters reported that the case was brought against Meta and EssilorLuxottica, the owner of the Ray-Ban brand.

The groups argue that the glasses can allow people to film others without their knowledge and may violate European privacy protections. Meta disputes the allegations.

## What are the groups asking the court to do?

The organisations include the Netherlands' main consumer association, Consumentenbond, and Offlimits, which campaigns against online sexual abuse.

They argue that the glasses create a situation in which people in public spaces may be recorded without knowing when recording is taking place or how footage is handled.

The case is before the Amsterdam District Court. Reuters reported that a decision was expected in mid-December, but no final ruling had been issued at the time of the report.

The request for a sales halt is not itself a court order. The distinction matters: the case is ongoing and the allegations have not been established as findings.

## What does Meta say?

Meta has disputed the allegations. Reuters reported that the company denied claims that it processes images recorded by the glasses in Europe or uses them to train AI models in violation of European law.

EssilorLuxottica's lawyer argued that the company neither processes nor has access to footage and that a sales ban would be disproportionate.

The case therefore involves competing claims about how recording, processing and responsibility are handled across the product and its supply chain.

## Why AI glasses raise a different privacy problem

A smartphone camera is visible in many recording situations. Smart glasses change the social signal.

The camera is built into an object that looks like ordinary eyewear. Even when a recording indicator is present, bystanders may not notice it or understand what it means.

There are several privacy questions:

- Can a person recognise when recording is taking place?
- How is footage stored, processed and transmitted?
- Can recorded material be used to improve AI systems?
- Who is responsible when footage includes people who have not consented?
- What rights do bystanders have to request deletion or challenge processing?

These questions extend beyond Meta. They apply to any wearable device that combines cameras, microphones and AI services.

## This is not an isolated European concern

Reuters reports that the debate is spreading across Europe and other markets. German consumer groups have also announced legal action, while the European Data Protection Board has warned about privacy risks associated with smart glasses.

The Dutch regulator has advised consumers against buying smart glasses, according to Reuters. Retail restrictions and venue bans have also emerged in some markets.

These developments show that privacy concerns are becoming a product adoption issue, not only a question for data protection lawyers.

## What this means for AI companies and device makers

AI hardware companies need to think about bystanders as well as the person wearing the device.

A privacy design should explain when recording is active, how data is processed, how long it is retained and whether it is used for model training. The explanation should be understandable to people who never agreed to use the product themselves.

For regulators, the challenge is applying existing privacy principles to devices that make recording and AI interaction continuous and less visible.

For users, the issue is simple: a product's convenience does not remove the privacy rights of people around them.

## What companies should review

1. Make recording indicators clear and difficult to overlook.
2. Explain local and cloud processing separately.
3. State whether recorded material can be used for model training.
4. Provide clear retention and deletion controls.
5. Assess the rights of bystanders and people captured incidentally.
6. Make product behaviour understandable without requiring users to read lengthy technical documentation.

A useful product metric is *privacy comprehension rate*: the percentage of users and test participants who can correctly explain when recording occurs, where footage goes and how it can be deleted.

## The privacy test for invisible AI

AI glasses make digital assistance more immediate, but they also make recording less obvious to the people nearby.

The long-term success of wearable AI will depend on more than battery life, model quality or design. People must be able to understand when they are being recorded and what happens to that information.
