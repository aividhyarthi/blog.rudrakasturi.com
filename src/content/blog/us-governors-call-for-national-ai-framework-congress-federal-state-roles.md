# US Governors Call for a National AI Framework. Their Proposal Defines Federal and State Roles

The National Governors Association (NGA) has called for a coordinated national approach to artificial intelligence policy in the United States. In principles announced on November 22, 2026, its bipartisan Governors Council to Secure American AI Dominance asked Congress to develop a federal framework while preserving an appropriate role for state governments.

The recommendations address innovation, public safety, workers, consumers, children, privacy and the division of regulatory responsibility between Washington and individual states.

The document is a policy proposal. It does not create new law or establish binding requirements for AI developers.

## What has the governors' association proposed?

The principles were developed by the NGA's bipartisan council, led by Maryland Governor Wes Moore, Indiana Governor Mike Braun, North Carolina Governor Josh Stein and Utah Governor Spencer Cox.

The association argues that the United States needs a national approach that avoids unnecessary conflicts between state rules while allowing states to address issues within their responsibilities.

Its recommendations cover six broad areas.

## The six policy priorities

| Priority | What the governors are asking for |
|---|---|
| Innovation and public protection | Support AI development while protecting public safety, civil rights and privacy |
| Workers | Prepare for workplace changes and support people affected by AI deployment |
| Federal and state roles | Federal government to address national and international risks; states to retain appropriate authority over deployment and local protections |
| Evidence-based safeguards | Match requirements to credible risks and use technically informed definitions |
| Regulatory consistency | Reduce unnecessary duplication and conflicting requirements |
| Competition | Avoid rules that unfairly favour large companies or prevent smaller firms from entering the market |

The recommendations are intended to inform Congress as it considers a national AI framework.

## Why is the federal-state question important?

The United States has a federal system in which national and state governments share responsibilities. AI products can operate across state borders, while many of their effects are experienced locally.

A national rule may provide consistency for developers and businesses. State governments, however, may want to address specific concerns involving consumers, children, workers and public services.

The NGA's proposal attempts to draw a line between these responsibilities.

| Policy area | Role proposed by the NGA |
|---|---|
| National and international AI risks | Federal government |
| Local deployment and public protections | State governments within their authority |
| Consumer and child safeguards | Coordinated action, with state flexibility |
| Workforce readiness | State and federal cooperation |
| Technical risk standards | Evidence-based and regularly reviewed requirements |

This is the association's recommended division of responsibilities, not a settled interpretation of existing law.

## What does the proposal say about workers?

The governors argue that workforce effects should be part of AI policy from the beginning. They call for states to prepare workers for changes in employment and to develop programmes that support people facing job transitions.

The proposal does not set a specific national retraining budget or prescribe a single workforce programme. It establishes worker readiness as a policy priority.

## What does it say about competition?

The NGA says AI regulation should not create unnecessary barriers for smaller companies or favour large technology firms.

This is an important part of the debate. Compliance requirements can improve safety, but complex obligations may also be easier for large companies to absorb than for startups and smaller developers.

The governors therefore recommend safeguards that are proportionate to credible risks and that are reviewed as technology changes.

## What happens next?

The principles are intended to inform Congress and discussions with the White House and industry. The announcement does not specify a legislative timetable, and it does not mean that Congress has accepted the recommendations.

| Question | Current position |
|---|---|
| Has the NGA published principles? | Yes, November 22, 2026 |
| Are they bipartisan? | Yes, the council is described as bipartisan |
| Are they federal law? | No |
| Has Congress adopted the framework? | Not established by this announcement |
| Is a national legislative timetable confirmed? | Not specified in the announcement |

## Why this proposal matters

The central policy question is not whether AI should be regulated. It is how responsibilities should be divided, how rules should remain consistent across jurisdictions and how safeguards can be enforced without unnecessarily restricting competition.

The governors' announcement adds a bipartisan state-level position to the US debate. Its practical influence will depend on how Congress, the White House and individual states respond.
