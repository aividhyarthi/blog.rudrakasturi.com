# Genz Mode:  
how they actually pulled this off

Not the politics. Not the headlines. Just — how do a few hundred thousand kids organise a month-long protest with no boss, no budget, and half of them hiding it from their own parents?

Every single thing below got dismissed as "just a meme" the day it happened. Read it as a tactic instead, and the whole protest starts looking less like chaos and more like a plan nobody wrote down.

Field notes on the Jantar Mantar sit-in July 2026

01 · the mask

## The costume was the strategy

Start with the thing everyone wrote off first. Half the crowd at Jantar Mantar showed up in **cockroach masks** — the cheap kind, half prop, half meme. On paper it's just a bit. In practice it's doing three jobs at once: nobody's face is in the frame for a police camera, everybody looks like part of the same story for a phone camera, and the insult that started this whole thing gets worn like a badge every single day the protest runs. You couldn't design a better costume if you tried. Somebody basically already did.

🪳 mask = merch 🪳 mask = shield 🪳 mask = punchline

02 · the diaper drive

## They literally mailed diapers to a minister

This is the bit that shouldn't work and somehow does: organisers ran a **"Diaper Donation Drive"** — supporters wrote resignation demands on actual diapers and sent them to the education minister's office. It's crude, it's hilarious, it's built for a screenshot, and it made a serious grievance about exam leaks impossible to cover as anything other than news. A protest press release gets ignored. A pile of diapers with the minister's name on them does not.

why it works Humour travels further than outrage. Outrage needs context. A joke just needs a caption.

03 · steal their own move

## They banged the government's own drum back at it

Remember 2020, banging plates and spoons from balconies for frontline workers, on the Prime Minister's own call? This crowd brought that exact gesture back — steel plates, spoons, same clatter — except now it's aimed at the government that asked for it the first time. Nobody had to explain the reference. That's the whole point of a callback: if you were online in 2020, you get it in half a second, no caption required.

04 · roses for the cops

## Flowers, flags, and a copy of the constitution

Turn up to the news footage and you'll see it: protesters carrying the national flag, a copy of the Constitution, red roses handed straight to the police officers standing guard. Volunteers went round reminding everyone to stay disciplined, not to provoke anyone, and to keep their phones out and recording. That last instruction matters as much as the flowers — in a protest like this, the footage isn't a side effect, it's the actual defence. If something goes wrong, the story has to already be filmed, edited, and posted before anyone else gets to write it first.

05 · the account that wouldn't die

## Ban us and watch what happens

When the movement's first account got taken down over alleged security concerns, the founder didn't fight it — he just opened a new one and captioned it **"cockroaches don't die."** Within weeks the group had racked up close to 22 million followers on Instagram, more than double the ruling party's own count. You cannot ban your way out of a joke that's already this good. Every takedown just became the next post.

06 · the part nobody's posting

## The quiet half of this story

Here's the bit that doesn't make it into the news pieces, but is floating everywhere in group chats and comment sections if you're actually in this generation's orbit: a lot of kids weren't just hiding their faces from the police. They were hiding them from home. Stories keep circulating of parents restricting a kid's phone or WhatsApp for the week of the march, precisely to keep them away from Jantar Mantar — and of teenagers going anyway, keeping the mask on a little longer than they needed to, for an audience of exactly one household.

👩 Amma

at the library studying for retest 🙂4:43 PM

good. stay away from that Jantar Mantar nonsense4:44 PM

obviously 🐜4:44 PM

a chat that probably happened in a thousand homes that week

It's a strange kind of protest fatigue and protest courage sitting in the same body — genuinely worried parents who lived through their own version of the system and want their kid nowhere near a police barricade, and kids who feel like this is the one fight worth the risk, and lying about a library to go stand in a crowd for it. Nobody's the villain in that story. That tension is just what it costs to show up when you're still young enough that someone else can technically ground you.

07 · the sign game

## This generation doesn't write slogans. It writes punchlines

Every protest generation before this one had a chant. This one has a caption contest. Walk through Jantar Mantar and the placards read less like demands and more like a group chat that got printed out — puns, movie references, therapy-speak, cricket jokes, all aimed at the same exam scandal. Recreated a few of the best ones below, because half the reporting on this movement quotes the anger and completely skips the wit.

MY PAD IS MORE LEAK PROOF THAN NTA

A sanitary pad ad line, repurposed to roast the exam body that couldn't stop its own papers leaking. Ruthless and it rhymes.

CHAI BANAO, CHUTIYA NAHI

"Make tea, not fools of us" — a direct dig at the self-made-chaiwala origin story, flipped into an insult.

MAI HOO COCKROACH 🪳

The official party placard. Print it, laminate it, become the insult. Merch and manifesto in one line.

WE ASKED FOR MAKE IN INDIA.  
YOU GAVE US LEAK IN INDIA.

Takes the government's own flagship slogan and rhymes it straight back into an accusation.

SHIT. NEHRU NE PHIRSE PAPER LEAK KARA DIYA

Sarcasm aimed at a very familiar move — blaming a prime minister from the 1950s for a 2026 exam scandal.

IPL: INDIAN PAPER LEAK

Rebrands the exam controversy as a cricket league, complete with mock "emerging leaker" awards. Sports-fan brainrot, weaponised.

ABEY JALDI RESIGNATION DE, KAL PANVEL NIKALNA HAI

"Hurry up and resign, I've got a train to catch tomorrow" — treating a cabinet resignation like a chore on today's to-do list.

JINKO KHUD KE BAAP SE PYAAR NAHI MILA, WOH NETA KO APNA "PAW-PAW" BANA LETE HAIN #DADDYISSUES

Pop-therapy language turned into political commentary — literally diagnosing blind political loyalty as a daddy issue, hashtag included.

A couple of the signs on the ground went further than a punchline — straight-up profanity, sexualised jabs at the government, stuff that would never clear a press release. Not reproducing those here, but that edge is part of the honest picture too: this crowd isn't performing politeness for the cameras, and that rawness is exactly what makes it read as real anger and not stage-managed theatre.

closing thought

## They protest the way they post

Fast, funny, documented from six angles, coordinated with no single person in charge — and somehow still serious enough to sit through a hunger strike and a baton charge for it. Older generations keep looking for the moment this "turns into a real protest," missing that the diapers and the memes and the masks _were_ the real protest the entire time. That was never the warm-up act. That was the strategy.
