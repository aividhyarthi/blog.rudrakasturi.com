The latest episode of **Shark Tank India Season 5**, featuring aromatherapy brand founder Dr Manoj Das, is being discussed as a “brutal rejection.”

That is lazy framing.

What happened on that stage was not humiliation. It was correction.

India did not watch a startup fail.  
India watched an entire category get audited in public.

And it failed.

### This was never about aromatherapy

Let us be clear from the start. Aromatherapy is not the villain here. Wellness is not the villain. Even alternative medicine is not the villain.

The real issue is something far more uncomfortable.

India has normalised selling belief as science.

Add “Doctor” to your name.  
Add “ancient wisdom” to your pitch.  
Add “chemical free” to your label.  
Remove proof.  
Hope no one asks questions.

This formula works beautifully on Instagram.  
It collapses instantly in front of investors.

That collapse is what we saw.

### The Sharks did not reject the product. They rejected the risk.

Every serious investor asks one core question, whether spoken or not:

If this goes wrong, what exactly are we defending in court?

In this pitch, there was no clean answer.

The moment you imply healing, curing, or medical outcomes, you cross an invisible but very real line. You are no longer selling lifestyle products. You are entering regulated territory, whether you like it or not.

And regulation does not care about confidence.  
It cares about credentials.

### The “Doctor” problem India keeps avoiding

This episode exposed a truth most people do not want to say out loud.

India has a dangerous obsession with borrowed authority.

There is also an uncomfortable truth for consumers.

In the need for a cure, we believe almost anyone.

We stop asking basic questions. We stop checking credentials. We stop demanding proof.

That blind trust does two things. It wastes time and money for people who can least afford it. And it quietly turns unverified sellers into powerful brands they do not deserve to be.

Bad wellness brands do not grow only because they market well. They grow because we reward belief faster than evidence.

The prefix “Dr” carries enormous weight in this country. It triggers trust instantly, especially in wellness and health. That trust can either be earned or exploited.

When credentials are unclear, loosely defined, or strategically vague, the brand is not just misleading consumers. It is manufacturing legitimacy.

On television, that might look like confidence.  
In a courtroom, it looks like liability.

No investor wants to fund that ambiguity.

### Claims are cheap. Proof is expensive.

One of the most damaging habits in Indian wellness startups is overclaiming.

Founders casually move from “this helps relaxation” to “this can treat conditions” without realising the implication. The moment you cross into medical language, the burden of proof explodes.

Clinical trials.  
Documented studies.  
Regulatory approvals.  
Clear disclaimers.

Most brands skip all of this and jump straight to storytelling.

Stories attract customers.  
Evidence attracts capital.

On Shark Tank, evidence wins. Stories get shredded.

### The valuation was not the real joke

Yes, the valuation raised eyebrows. But India has funded worse numbers with far weaker fundamentals.

Valuation was not the deal breaker.

Defensibility was.

A hundred crore business needs at least one solid moat. Proprietary formulation. Strong intellectual property. Distribution dominance. Or terrifyingly good retention data.

What was presented instead was belief backed by confidence.

Belief does not scale.  
Proof does.

### Why this episode matters more than it seems

This was not just reality TV drama. This was a signal.

A signal that the Indian startup ecosystem is done romanticising unverified wellness.

Investors are getting stricter.  
Regulators are paying attention.  
Consumers are no longer blindly trusting labels.  
Influencer driven pseudo science is losing its free pass.

This episode will quietly change how pitches are prepared in this category.

And that is a good thing.

### The irony nobody is discussing

Globally, the most successful wellness brands follow a boring but powerful rule.

They underclaim and overdeliver.

They sell calm, not cures.  
Support, not salvation.  
Experience, not medicine.

In India, we do the opposite.

We promise miracles.  
Then get offended when questioned.

On Shark Tank, questions are the product.

### This was not brutality. This was overdue honesty.

Calling this episode “harsh” misses the point.

What actually happened was long delayed accountability.

For years, Indian wellness has operated in a grey zone where vibes replaced validation and confidence replaced compliance.

That era is ending.

And it should.

### The real lesson for founders

If you are building in wellness, health, beauty, or anything adjacent, this episode is your warning label.

Be precise about credentials.  
Be conservative with claims.  
Separate lifestyle from medical clearly.  
Invest in proof before marketing.

Titles do not build trust anymore.  
Transparency does.

### The line that sums it all up

Shark Tank India did not reject a founder.  
It rejected India’s habit of selling belief as science.

And that rejection was necessary.

Because the next generation of Indian consumer brands will not be built on borrowed authority.

They will be built on evidence.

And that is how real trust scales.
