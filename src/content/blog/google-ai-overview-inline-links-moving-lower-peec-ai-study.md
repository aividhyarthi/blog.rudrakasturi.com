**Short answer:** Peec AI researcher David Konitzny reports that the share of AI Overviews with the first inline link in the opening third of the answer fell from 76.8% on September 21 to 51.8% on November 20. This is third-party link-position data, not a measure of clicks or publisher traffic.

Google has spent 2026 making links inside AI answers more visible. But new third-party data suggests that the first clickable link inside many AI Overviews may now appear further down the response than it did two months ago.

David Konitzny of Peec AI shared an analysis on November 24. He reported that 76.8% of Google AI Overviews had their first inline link in the first third of the answer on September 21. By November 20, that share had fallen to 51.8%.

That is a decline of 25 percentage points. It is an interesting change in link placement, but it is not evidence that clicks to publishers have fallen by the same amount. The data tracks where the first inline link appears inside an AI answer, not whether a user clicked it.

## What did the Peec AI analysis find?

Konitzny says he analysed more than 88 million chats to track the position of the first inline link in Google AI Overviews.

The two figures he shared are:

| Date | AI Overviews with first inline link in the first third of the answer |
|---|---:|
| September 21, 2026 | 76.8% |
| November 20, 2026 | 51.8% |
| Change | Down 25 percentage points |

He also identified two noticeable drops around October 9 and October 30.

The important detail is what the analysis counts. These are inline links placed directly inside the answer text. They are not the source cards shown beside an answer or the citation list that may appear below it.

Search Engine Roundtable reported the findings on November 24 and linked to Konitzny's public post.

## Why does link position matter?

A link near the beginning of an answer may be easier for a user to notice while reading. A link that appears much later may require the person to scroll through more of the generated response.

That creates a possible difference in visibility even when a page is technically cited.

This matters because publishers often report AI citations as a single number. A page cited in the first paragraph and a page linked near the end of a long answer both count as citations, but they may not have the same chance of being noticed.

Google itself said in an October 15 announcement that it was making inline links appear higher in AI responses more frequently. The Peec AI observation is therefore worth watching, but it should not be presented as proof that Google reversed that product change. The two sources describe different things: Google's stated product direction and an external researcher's measurement of observed link positions over time.

## What the data does not prove

There are several limits publishers should keep in mind.

**It does not measure clicks.** The figures show the position of the first inline link, not the number of people who clicked it.

**It does not measure publisher traffic.** A link can appear in an answer without generating a visit. Traffic needs to be measured separately in analytics and Search Console.

**It is not a Google dataset.** The numbers were shared by a researcher at Peec AI. Google has not published these percentages as an official metric.

**It does not describe every link format.** The analysis concerns inline links inside answer text. It should not be mixed with side cards, source panels or links at the end of an AI response.

**It does not establish the cause.** The observation does not tell us whether the change came from answer length, interface experiments, query mix, model behaviour or another factor.

These limits do not make the research useless. They tell us how to interpret it without turning an observation into a claim about traffic.

## What should publishers measure now?

A useful AI visibility report needs more than a count of citations.

1. **Record the exact cited URL.** A domain-level mention does not tell you which article received visibility. Our [guide to tracking cited URLs across AI platforms](/what-are-cited-urls-ai-search-track-chatgpt-gemini-perplexity-2026/) explains a repeatable page-level method.
2. **Capture the link format.** Record whether the link appeared inline, in a source card, in a carousel or at the end of the response.
3. **Record the position.** Note whether the first link appeared in the opening third, middle third or final third of the answer.
4. **Save the full response.** Keep the prompt, platform, date, language, location context and screenshot or permitted export.
5. **Check the destination.** Confirm that the cited page actually supports the claim attached to it.
6. **Measure clicks separately.** Use referral analytics and Search Console data where available. Do not treat a citation as a visit.
7. **Repeat the same prompts.** Compare like with like over time. A different prompt set can change the result even when the platform has not changed.

A useful metric is an *early inline citation rate*: the percentage of tested answers in which the first inline link appears in the opening third of the answer. Keep it separate from citation frequency, referral visits and conversions.

## Should publishers rewrite articles to get an earlier citation?

There is no evidence in this dataset that a particular writing format causes Google to place a link higher in an answer.

Still, publishers should make the main answer and supporting evidence easy to find. State the central fact clearly, attribute it, provide original context and link to primary sources. Avoid hiding the most important information beneath a long introduction.

That is good editorial practice, not a guaranteed way to influence citation position.

Our guide to [Google's explanation of how AI Overviews and AI Mode select sources](/google-explains-how-ai-overviews-and-ai-mode-select-and-link-sources/) explains the process Google has documented. It says that Search ranking, retrieval, grounding and the usefulness of a link all play a role in source selection and display.

## The metric publishers should add to AI reporting

The Peec AI finding points to a gap in many AI visibility dashboards. They record whether a page was cited, but not where the link appeared or whether it sent a visitor.

Track four separate measures:

- **Citation rate:** how often a page appears as a visible source in a fixed set of tests.
- **Early inline citation rate:** how often the first inline link appears in the opening third of the answer.
- **Referral rate:** how often those answers result in identifiable visits.
- **Outcome rate:** how often those visits lead to a registration, enquiry, subscription or sale.

These measures answer different questions. A publisher may gain citations without gaining clicks. It may also receive fewer visits but attract a more relevant audience.

The current data is a signal worth monitoring, not a verdict on AI Search traffic. The next step is to measure link placement and actual visits independently, using a consistent prompt set and a transparent method.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are Google AI Overviews showing fewer links?

The cited Peec AI analysis measures the position of the first inline link, not the total number of links. It reports that the first inline link appeared in the opening third less often in November than in September.

### Does the Peec AI data prove that publisher clicks have fallen?

No. It measures link placement in observed answers. It does not measure whether users clicked those links or whether publishers lost traffic.

### Is this an official Google metric?

No. The percentages were shared by David Konitzny of Peec AI. Google has not published these figures as an official Search metric.

### Are inline citations the same as source cards?

No. Inline citations appear within the generated answer text. Source cards, carousels and panels are different link placements and should be recorded separately.

### Why should publishers track citation position?

A link near the start of an answer may be easier to notice than one much further down. Position adds context to a citation count, although it does not by itself prove user attention or clicks.

### What is an early inline citation rate?

It is a measurement a publisher can define as the share of tested answers where the first inline link appears in the opening third. Keep it separate from citation frequency, visits and conversions.
