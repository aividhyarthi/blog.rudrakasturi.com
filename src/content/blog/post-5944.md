## Why This Is a Branding and ORM Play, Not a Revenue Story

Every few months, the internet falls in love with a number.

This time, it is ₹38 crore.

According to a widely circulated media PR about an Indian AI-driven YouTube channel **Bandar Apna Dost** has become the “biggest AI channel on YouTube” and is supposedly earning close to $4 million a year.

People are impressed.  
People are inspired.  
People are also misled.

Let me be very clear.

This is not a revenue story.  
This is a branding, ORM, and perception story.

And those two are very different things.

## The first rule of YouTube money

Views do not equal value

The channel in question has around **256 crore lifetime views**.  
That number looks massive until you ask one simple question.

Where did those views come from?

The answer matters more than the count.

Most of these views are:

*   From YouTube Shorts
*   From India
*   From mass entertainment content with no buying intent

That combination alone collapses the ₹38 crore claim.

## Let us talk about RPM. The boring truth nobody puts in headlines

RPM means revenue per 1,000 views.  
This is the only number that matters in YouTube monetisation.

In India, Shorts RPM typically sits between:  
₹0.80 to ₹4 per 1,000 views

And that is on a good day.

Now apply this to reality.

256 crore views equals roughly 2.56 billion views.

Even if we assume a **very generous** ₹4 RPM:

2.56 billion ÷ 1,000 × ₹4  
That gives you roughly **₹10 crore lifetime revenue**.

Not yearly.  
Lifetime.

Now apply real-world friction:

*   Not all views are monetised
*   Shorts RPM often trends closer to ₹1–₹2
*   Tier 2 and Tier 3 audiences pull RPM down further

The number quickly drops to **₹20–40 lakh territory**.

That is not failure.  
That is just how India’s ad market works.

## Why AI meme and Shorts channels earn even less

There is a term advertisers use quietly.

Low commercial intent content.

AI meme style Shorts, looping narratives, and universal humour fall into this bucket.

Advertisers love:

*   Finance
*   Education
*   Career
*   SaaS
*   High intent explainers

They avoid:

*   Repetitive Shorts
*   AI-generated meme formats
*   Content with no clear buyer profile

So the RPM does not scale with virality.  
It compresses.

Being watched by everyone often means being valuable to no specific advertiser.

## Then how do these creators “make money”?

This is where branding comes in.

Not YouTube ads.

### Brand integrations

Such channels may attract:

*   Gaming apps
*   OTT platforms
*   Consumer apps
*   Low-cost mass brands

Typical Indian brand deal range:  
₹50,000 to ₹3 lakh per integration

Even with aggressive assumptions:

*   Two deals per month
*   ₹2 lakh per deal

That is **₹48 lakh per year**.

Again, respectable.  
Again, nowhere near ₹38 crore.

## So why push a ₹38 crore narrative?

Because perception compounds faster than revenue.

This is classic ORM thinking.

### What the headline actually does

It does not inform.  
It positions.

It tells:

*   Brands that this creator is “big money”
*   Platforms that this channel is “important”
*   Critics that questioning quality is pointless

It creates an anchor.

Once people believe someone is rich, they stop questioning how.

## This is not accidental. It is strategic

In the creator economy, attention precedes income.

A story like this:

*   Increases search volume
*   Triggers algorithmic curiosity
*   Invites agency outreach
*   Converts virality into credibility

Whether the number is accurate becomes secondary.

The story does the work.

## This does not mean the creator failed

Let me be clear.

Going viral at scale is hard.  
Building distribution is real skill.  
Understanding Shorts psychology is valuable.

But confusing **reach** with **revenue** is dangerous.

Especially for young creators who believe:  
“If I go viral, money will follow.”

In India, it often does not.

## The uncomfortable truth

India is a low RPM market.  
Shorts are a low intent format.  
AI mass content is a low advertiser trust category.

Put them together and you get:

*   Massive visibility
*   Modest income
*   Powerful branding leverage

That is the real story here.

> If anything, this episode is a useful case study for how attention, perception, and monetisation are often confused in the creator economy, not just by audiences, but sometimes by media coverage itself.

## My final take

This ₹38 crore narrative is not about money earned.

It is about:

*   Authority borrower
*   Perception engineered
*   ORM executed well

The creator may be winning the attention game.  
The PR team is definitely winning the perception game.

But the revenue math does not support the myth. And in the long run, clarity always outperforms hype.

**_Note:_** _**This is not an argument about whether the creator is talented or deserving. Virality at this scale takes skill. What this piece examines is how revenue narratives are constructed around viral success, and why those narratives often collapse under basic monetisation math, especially in the Indian Shorts ecosystem.**_
