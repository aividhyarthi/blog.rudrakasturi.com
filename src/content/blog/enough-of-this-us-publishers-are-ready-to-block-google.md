# Google Zero, Everywhere at Once

American publishers are threatening to block Google entirely. It's a live option for them because they have somewhere else to send their readers. In India, where Google runs 97% of search, that same threat is a lot harder to make.

RUDRA PRASAD KASTURI · rudrakasturi.com

  
  

This week the [Wall Street Journal reported](https://www.wsj.com/business/media/google-search-publishers-ai-content-0fb06e41) that some of the biggest names in American publishing - USA Today, Politico, Reuters, People, and reportedly even Reddit - are seriously discussing whether to block Google's crawlers entirely. Not tweak their SEO. Not diversify traffic. _Cut the cord._ It's a startling escalation, and it's tempting to read it as the headline of a single, global story: publishers vs. Google, AI edition. It isn't. Whether "blocking Google" is even a real option depends entirely on how monopolized search already is in your market - and that number swings harder by country than most of this coverage lets on.

USA Today's CEO can talk about blocking Google because Bing, Yahoo, and AI chat referrals give American readers a real second door. **Indian readers barely have one** - Bing sits near 1–2% of the market, and there's no meaningful non-Google alternative at Indian scale. Same threat, completely different amount of leverage behind it.

## What's actually happening in the US

The trigger everywhere is the same: AI Overviews and AI Mode answer the query on Google's own results page, using the publisher's own reporting, and the reader never has to click through. The bargain that held the open web together for two decades - "let us crawl and summarize you, and we'll send you an audience" - is breaking because the second half keeps shrinking.

It's time to take a stand and say enough is enough. Mike Reed, CEO, USA Today Co.

Turning them off and blocking them entirely is 100% on the table. Neil Vogel, CEO, People Inc.

Reuters is weighing the trade-off for its consumer product specifically, while leaning on B2B revenue elsewhere. Politico staff have discussed limiting bot access to free articles. Time now designs for two separate audiences on one site - humans who show up, and bots who don't leave anything behind. Reddit, whose forums quietly power a large share of Google's results, has reportedly discussed cutting AI access entirely, right as its own data-licensing deal comes up for renewal. Not everyone agrees blocking is the answer: the Economist's line is that "traffic can be choppy and declining, but traffic is still traffic" - leaving Google isn't currently on their table at all.

Global picture

## This isn't just an American story

The Reuters Institute's 2026 industry survey of 280 media leaders across 51 countries found Google search referrals down roughly a third globally over the year to November 2025, with publishers now expecting a further 43% decline by 2029 - a fifth of respondents expect losses above 75%. Zero-click search, where the reader never leaves Google at all, is now close to 69% for news-related queries specifically. The UK's DMG Media, which owns MailOnline and Metro, has documented declines as steep as 89% for some search terms. Social platforms haven't picked up the slack either: X and Facebook referrals are roughly flat globally, with real growth concentrated in the US alone.

India

## Why "just block Google" is a much harder sentence to say here

I spent close to two decades on the inside of Indian digital newsrooms watching search referral dashboards the way a trader watches a ticker. Search wasn't a channel; it was very often the largest single source of readers a title had, full stop. Reading the WSJ story from Rajahmundry rather than New York changes what it means.

Google runs roughly 97–98% of all search in India - among the very highest concentration of any major market in the world, well above the US, and nowhere close to Germany or Japan, where meaningful alternatives exist. There's no Bing-with-Copilot moment coming for Indian publishers the way there has for Japan. Trade press has already reported Indian publishers quietly exploring collective responses to the same AI Overviews traffic loss playing out in the US - but "collective response" in an ecosystem this concentrated looks very different from "block the crawler and walk away." For most Indian titles, that's not a boardroom option; it's closer to switching off the lights.

### The one genuine cushion: vernacular and regional content

Where Indian publishers do have real short-term insulation, it's in regional-language and hyperlocal content - Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, and beyond. AI Overviews and answer engines are still visibly weaker at summarizing vernacular queries cleanly than English ones, and India's own digital ad and content growth is tilting hard toward exactly this content. That's a genuine window, not a permanent moat - but it's the one lever Indian publishers have that most Western newsrooms don't.

### The dependency cuts both ways

Because Google is so dominant here, it also has more to lose from a coordinated Indian publisher response than the numbers above might suggest - India is Google's largest market by user count anywhere in the world. That's leverage too, just not the kind that gets exercised by unilaterally blocking a crawler. It's the kind that gets exercised in negotiation, in regulatory attention, and in how aggressively AI Overviews gets rolled out for Indian-language and news queries specifically.

### For any publisher or growth team, wherever you sit

1.  **Instrument for both audiences.** Track human sessions and AI-crawler/citation activity separately - they're no longer the same funnel and shouldn't be reported as one metric.
2.  **Build the direct relationship an AI summary can't intercept.** App notifications, WhatsApp/newsletter distribution, and habitual direct-type-in traffic are the channels that survive a zero-click world.
3.  **Model before you block.** Every publisher quoted in the WSJ piece is running the actual traffic-value math first, not acting on instinct. Do the same before treating "block Google" as a strategy rather than a threat.
4.  **Treat citability as a content requirement.** Clear sourcing, original data, and quotable structure are what gets a publisher surfaced inside an AI answer instead of erased by one.

### Specifically for Indian digital media

1.  **Don't benchmark your exposure against US publishers.** A 97% Google-dependent market has far less room to threaten, block, or negotiate from strength than an 85% one does - plan accordingly.
2.  **Double down on the vernacular window while it's open.** Regional-language depth is the one advantage that doesn't exist for most Western newsrooms right now - it won't last forever as AI answer quality in Indian languages improves.
3.  **Push distribution outside the browser entirely.** App-first and WhatsApp-native distribution matter more in India's mobile-first market than almost anywhere else - that's infrastructure Google's AI Overview can't sit on top of.

Whether or not any of the US publishers named in the WSJ story actually flip the switch, the fact that it's a live boardroom conversation at USA Today, Reuters, and People is the real headline. But the more useful question for anyone building media in India isn't "should we block Google too" - it's "what do we build now, given that we can't."
