Google Ads is designed to match a keyword with searches that are relevant to what a person wants, not only searches that repeat the exact words an advertiser entered. That gives campaigns room to reach people who use different wording. It can also bring in searches that are less likely to become customers.

A study published by advertising agency New Path Digital offers a useful example. It examined nearly 50,000 keyword-and-search-term pairs from three lead-generation accounts. The agency found that searches newly matched to phrase-match keywords cost 28% more per lead than searches those same keywords had already matched.

The finding deserves attention, but it needs context. It is a study of three accounts, not a Google-wide benchmark. It measures form-fill leads, not necessarily qualified leads or paying customers.

## What did the study find?

New Path Digital compared the searches its clients' keywords matched in 2022 and 2023 with the searches those same keywords matched in 2025 and 2026.

The study covered three lead-generation advertisers: a law firm, a higher-education advertiser and a local business-to-business service company. Across the accounts, the agency analysed 49,904 keyword-and-search-term pairs.

Its account-mix-adjusted figures showed:

| Match type | Existing searches | Newly matched searches | Difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Phrase match cost per lead | $163 | $209 | 28% higher |
| Exact match cost per lead | $116 | $109 | 7% lower |

The phrase-match result is the headline finding. The newly matched searches produced form-fill leads at $209 each, compared with $163 for searches that the same keywords had already matched.

The exact-match result moved in the opposite direction. Newly matched exact-match searches cost 7% less per lead in the study.

## Why did new phrase-match searches cost more?

The difference did not come from a higher average click price.

The study reported an average click price of about $16 for both existing and newly matched phrase searches. The conversion rate, however, fell from 10% for existing searches to 8% for new searches.

In simple terms, advertisers paid roughly the same amount for a click, but a smaller share of the new searches became form fills. That pushed the cost per lead higher.

The study also found that phrase-match searches sharing no words with the original keyword increased from 2% to 9% of volume. That does not automatically make those searches irrelevant. A person can express the same intent using different words. But it shows why advertisers should inspect the actual search terms and the quality of the leads they generate.

## What does Google mean by phrase match?

Phrase match is one of Google's keyword matching options. It can show an ad when a search includes the meaning of a keyword, including searches that express the same intent in different wording. It is broader than exact match, while generally more restrictive than broad match.

For example, an advertiser bidding on a phrase-match keyword such as "online accounting software" may reach searches that use related wording or express a similar need. The precise searches depend on context and Google's matching systems.

This is why a keyword report alone is not enough. The keyword tells you what the advertiser selected. The search-terms report helps show what people actually typed and which searches triggered the ad.

## What the 28% figure does not prove

The study is useful because it uses real account data. It is not a controlled experiment that proves phrase match itself caused every difference.

There are several limitations.

**Only three accounts were included.** The advertisers were a law firm, a higher-education business and a local B2B service provider. Ecommerce, travel, finance, local retail and other sectors may behave differently.

**The conversion was a form fill.** A submitted form is not necessarily a qualified prospect, a sales opportunity or a customer. The study does not establish the cost per closed sale.

**The periods were different.** The comparison used searches matched in 2022-23 and 2025-26. Competition, user behaviour, campaigns and market conditions can change over time.

**Some search terms were hidden.** PPC Land's account of the study says nearly 43% of eligible search terms were unavailable, mostly in phrase match. That limits what can be inferred about the complete search mix.

**The figures were adjusted for account mix.** New Path Digital weighted the new-query results to account for differences in how much each advertiser contributed to the existing-query conversions. This makes the comparison more useful, but it is still the agency's analysis of its own clients.

The right conclusion is not that phrase match is bad. It is that newly matched searches can perform differently from the searches a keyword has historically attracted.

## What should Google Ads advertisers check?

The study points to a practical audit that applies to Indian businesses as well as advertisers in other markets.

1. **Open the search-terms report.** Review the actual queries that triggered phrase-match keywords. Do not rely only on the keyword list.
2. **Separate existing and newly appearing intent.** Identify searches that have recently started matching your established keywords.
3. **Check lead quality.** Connect form fills with CRM stages, qualified leads, sales calls and closed revenue wherever possible.
4. **Review negative keywords.** Exclude searches that are clearly unrelated to the product, service, customer or geography you serve.
5. **Compare match types fairly.** Evaluate phrase and exact match using the same conversion definitions and comparable time periods.
6. **Look beyond cost per lead.** A cheaper lead that never converts into revenue can be more expensive than a higher-cost qualified lead.
7. **Avoid blanket changes.** Do not pause every phrase-match keyword because one study found a higher cost in three accounts.

For Indian lead-generation campaigns, this matters in categories such as education, real estate, financial services, healthcare and B2B software. A campaign may generate many enquiries at an attractive cost while sending sales teams a large volume of people who are not eligible, ready or relevant.

The useful metric is not simply cost per form fill. It is cost per qualified lead and, where the data allows, cost per customer.

## A simple search-term quality score

Teams can make the review more consistent by scoring each new search term against four checks:

| Check | Score |
|---|---:|
| Does the search match the product or service? | 0 to 2 |
| Does it show the right customer intent? | 0 to 2 |
| Is the location or audience relevant? | 0 to 2 |
| Does it produce qualified leads or sales? | 0 to 2 |

A search term can score from zero to eight. This is a suggested internal audit method, not a Google Ads metric. Review low-scoring terms first, then decide whether to add negatives, separate them into another ad group or keep them because they contribute valuable conversions.

## What this means for automated advertising

The larger change is that keyword matching increasingly depends on meaning and context. That can help advertisers discover demand they would not have captured through a narrow list of exact phrases. It also makes measurement and feedback quality more important.

If Google receives only a form submission as a conversion signal, it may not know whether that enquiry became a genuine business opportunity. Advertisers should send accurate conversion information back to the platform where their systems and privacy obligations allow it.

The more automated the campaign becomes, the more important it is to define what a valuable conversion actually means.

## The takeaway

New Path Digital's study found a 28% higher cost per lead for newly matched phrase searches in three accounts. The increase was linked to a lower conversion rate, not a higher average click price. Newly matched exact searches performed differently, with a 7% lower cost per lead in the same study.

That is a reason to inspect search terms and lead quality, not a reason to abandon phrase match. Advertisers should use their own account data to decide whether broader matching is bringing in new customers or simply more form fills.

*Research note: The figures in this article come from New Path Digital's study of three managed lead-generation accounts. The results are not a universal benchmark and should not be interpreted as proof of causation.*
